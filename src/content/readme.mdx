## TL;DR

1. **Show me the mechanism.** How it works, not what it's called.
2. **Have an opinion.** Vague, hedged answers give me nothing to push on.
3. **Write it down.** A doc, repo, or diagram beats a slide deck.
4. **Argue with me.** I'll take either side to find the best answer. Lots of questions means I'm interested, not that I'm against it.
5. **I need ideas to land.** I'm happy in an optional seat if my input moves the work. I check out when it doesn't.

## Who I am

Lately I'm working on ways of working with AI systems: if you capture what your organization knows (the stuff that lives in people's heads) and build it into how your agents are set up, you get an advantage that lasts. I'm interested in the human factors that make AI adoptions successful. If you're using agents in real work, tell me what's working (or not). I also like talking about system design, agent protocols, developer platforms, and how organizations work (Deming, Scott, Toyota Kata).

## How I work

- **Mechanism first.** "It improves alignment" means nothing to me until you show how it works. Tell me the goal, the limits, and what's been tried, with real examples.
- **Source control is the source of truth.** Work should live in a repo as code, config, or "specs", and approvals should happen there.
- **Kill toil, don't codify it.** I try to remove busywork with well-designed systems and tools. Putting a review step into a workflow tool doesn't remove it.
- **Watch the handoffs.** Problems pile up between systems and teams, where nobody owns them.
- **Share rough work early.** I'd rather improve a working draft than polish a big reveal.
- **Match care to stakes.** If we can undo it, try it and learn. If we can't, know the downside and how we'd spot trouble.

## I argue to think

Arguing is how I test a tech choice, plan, or team direction. I'll argue either side to see which holds up. I do this more than most people I work with, so:

- If I argue a position, I may not hold it. Ask whether it's mine or devil's advocate.
- Arguing back is the most useful thing you can do, live or in writing later.
- I'll push back on a polished plan that misses the real problem. That's about the plan, not you.
- Tell me if I'm steamrolling.

## Communicating with me

- **Skip the slides.** Do the thinking in writing. A deck is fine afterward to present it.
- **Feedback:** give it to me straight, more direct than you're probably comfortable with. If I'm wrong, show me why and I'll change my mind. I'm happy to adapt to your style, so it doesn't have to be mutual. I'm not scary.
- **Tell me what you need from me:** a decision, a review, someone to think out loud with, or help doing the work.
- **Write first.** Send a message with context, and ask for a call if we need to talk it through. I'll push back on meetings that could be a doc.
- **If something isn't working,** tell me plainly what happened and what you'd like changed. If I ask questions, I'm trying to understand, not defend myself.

## What keeps me engaged

I do my best work when I'm learning something, on hard design problems, with a small, smart, curious team that has time to build things well (I love good UX). I don't need (or want) the spotlight, and I don't need to be the one writing the code. I lose interest in box-ticking, reports nobody reads, hype over quality, and having to play politics to be heard.

**If you're junior:** I like mentoring. Show me rough work and expect specific feedback.

## Known quirks

- **I fix first, explain later.** If you need me to share an idea sooner, say so.
